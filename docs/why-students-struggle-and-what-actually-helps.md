# Learning to program is hard: why students struggle and what actually helps

**Programming education faces a fundamental challenge: approximately half of introductory students fail or withdraw**, with rates of 47-50% documented across institutions worldwide. This literature review synthesizes computer science education research to explain why programming presents unique cognitive demands, what struggling students experience internally, which interventions work, and how certain dispositions disadvantage learners. The evidence points to a clear conclusion: programming difficulty stems not primarily from syntax mastery but from the requirement to build accurate mental models of invisible computational processes while managing high cognitive load and often counterproductive beliefs about innate talent.

## Programming demands invisible reasoning that other subjects do not require

The central challenge in learning to program lies in constructing what researchers call the **notional machine**—an abstract mental model of how computers execute code step by step. Unlike physics, where students observe phenomena, or mathematics, where equations behave according to visible rules, programmers must reason about processes they cannot directly see (Sorva, 2013). Students must simultaneously understand static code, dynamic runtime behavior, and memory states—switching constantly between what the code says and what the machine does.

This cognitive demand creates what Lahtinen et al. (2005) identified in their international survey of 500+ students: "Novice programmers usually fail to combine abstract concepts and knowledge into program design, even though they know the syntax and semantics of individual statements." The problem is not learning individual constructs but orchestrating them into working programs. A large-scale SIGCSE 2023 analysis of **45,000 code snapshots** confirmed that many students simply "write code without decomposing"—they lack the strategic knowledge to break problems into manageable parts.

Research consistently identifies a hierarchy of difficulty. Syntax errors, while frustrating, are relatively easy to fix—Altadmri and Brown's landmark 2015 study of **37 million compilations** from 250,000+ students found median syntax error resolution under 30 seconds. The real obstacles are semantic and logical errors that compile but produce wrong behavior. These provide no automated feedback, requiring students to compare expected versus actual behavior manually. Ahmadzadeh et al. (2005) found that even students with "good understanding of programming do not acquire skills to debug programs effectively."

The research reveals consistent misconception patterns that transcend programming languages. Kaczmarczyk et al. (2010), in a SIGCSE paper voted among the top contributions from 50 years of the conference, identified four major themes: students misunderstand how variables are stored in memory, confuse assignment with mathematical equality, struggle with reference versus value semantics, and apply natural language expectations to code (the "superbug" phenomenon). For object-oriented programming specifically, Holland et al. (1997) documented persistent confusion between classes and objects, misunderstanding of reference aliasing, and incorrect models of inheritance.

A troubling finding from Brown and Altadmri (2017) is that **educators' beliefs about common student errors poorly match actual error data**. Teachers showed only "moderate correspondence" between their rankings and measured error frequencies—an expert blind spot that means instruction often targets the wrong problems.

## Struggling learners experience cognitive overload and emotional volatility

The internal experience of a struggling novice programmer is characterized by working memory overload and predictable emotional cycles. Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory, applied extensively to programming education (Berssanette & De Francisco, 2022), explains why: programming requires holding multiple types of information simultaneously—syntax rules, semantic meaning, problem-solving strategy, and debugging logic—while working memory can only manage **3-5 chunks** of information at once. Poorly designed instructional materials compound this through the split-attention effect, forcing students to mentally integrate separate sources like code, output windows, and documentation.

D'Mello, Bosch, and colleagues tracked emotions in real-time during programming learning with 99 novices, finding a characteristic distribution: **engagement (23%), confusion (22%), frustration (14%), and boredom (12%)**. More importantly, they mapped affect dynamics—predictable transitions between states. Engagement and confusion are reciprocally linked, as are confusion and frustration. When frustration persists, it transitions one-way to boredom. Their work established that confusion-to-frustration-to-boredom represents a danger zone for learning, while boredom-to-engagement transitions correlate positively with outcomes.

D'Mello, Lehman, Pekrun, and Graesser (2014) showed confusion can be beneficial—but only within what Graesser called the "Zone of Proximal Confusion." Productive confusion is brief, prompts active resolution attempts, and receives timely support. Unproductive confusion persists, transitions to frustration, and leads to disengagement. Liu et al. (2013) found that brief sequences of confusion plus frustration aided learning, but prolonged sequences predicted poor outcomes.

Metacognitive research reveals another challenge: **struggling students systematically overestimate their understanding** while high-performing students underestimate theirs. Prather, Becker, and colleagues' comprehensive systematic review in ACM TOCE documented inaccurate judgments of learning throughout programming education. Students believe rote memorization and re-reading are effective strategies when they are not. This mirrors the Dunning-Kruger effect, making struggling learners particularly poor at recognizing when they need help.

Self-efficacy emerges as perhaps the strongest predictor of persistence and performance. Ramalingam and Wiedenbeck's foundational scale development work showed that prior programming experience shapes self-efficacy, which in turn shapes mental models and course performance. Tsai, Wang, and Hsu (2019) confirmed that sufficient self-efficacy predicts higher confidence in overcoming problems. Critically, self-efficacy is malleable through mastery experiences—Allaire-Duquette et al. (2022) found that gender differences in programming self-efficacy disappeared after just a two-hour hands-on robotics workshop.

Imposter syndrome affects **57% of CS students** according to Rosenstein, Raghu, and Porter's 2020 SIGCSE study using the Clance IP Scale—significantly higher than other student populations. Women are disproportionately affected at **71% versus 52% for men**. Students experiencing imposter syndrome ask fewer questions, try fewer new approaches, and experience halted progress. They misinterpret normal learning struggles as evidence they "don't belong."

## Evidence-based interventions focus on scaffolding the path from reading to writing code

Research supports a clear pedagogical principle: **students should read and trace code before writing it**. Lister and colleagues established a skill hierarchy—knowledge of constructs leads to tracing ability, which supports explanation, then modification, and finally code generation. Students who cannot trace code effectively less than 50% of the time also cannot explain code effectively. Hertz and Jump's trace-based teaching model demonstrated that starting class with 20-30 minutes of tracing **increased attainment and decreased dropout rates**.

**Parsons problems**—code-block puzzles where students arrange pre-written lines into correct order—represent one of the best-supported interventions. The 2022 ITiCSE Working Group systematic review (Ericson et al.) concluded they are "at least as effective for learning computer science as writing freeform code" while being **30-50% faster to complete**. Barbara Ericson's dissertation showed adaptive Parsons problems made students nearly twice as likely to reach correct solutions. For struggling learners, the reduced cognitive load allows focus on program structure rather than syntax generation.

**Subgoal labeling**—grouping related steps under meaningful functional labels in worked examples—shows particular promise for at-risk students. Margulieux, Morrison, and Decker's semester-long study with 265 students found **lower dropout and failure rates** in the subgoal-labeled group. As Guzdial noted, "students most at risk of failing are the ones who gain the most." The technique works by helping students recognize patterns across problems rather than treating each as unique.

The **PRIMM framework** (Predict-Run-Investigate-Modify-Make) structures the transition from reading to writing through explicit phases. Sentance, Waite, and Kallia's 2019 quasi-experimental study with 493 students showed improved performance, with teachers particularly valuing PRIMM's ability to support **mixed-ability classes, enabling all learners to make progress**. The Predict phase—where students articulate what they think code will do before running it—serves as both diagnostic and learning activity.

**Peer instruction** produces dramatic results when properly implemented. Porter, Lee, and Simon's 2013 SIGCSE paper documented **halving failure rates** across four CS courses. A subsequent multi-institutional study (Porter et al., 2016 Best Paper) confirmed improved exam scores and increased retention. The mechanism involves displaying conceptual multiple-choice questions, having students vote individually, then discuss in pairs, then re-vote—with instructor explanation following. The peer discussion phase forces articulation of reasoning that helps surface and correct misconceptions.

**Debugging instruction** requires explicit teaching, not just practice. Zhang et al.'s 2025 longitudinal study found that context-specific instruction—combining abstract principles with code-specific worked examples—**dramatically outperformed** both no instruction and abstract guidelines alone, achieving 80% correctness after one session versus 20-44% for other approaches. Smith's 2023 dissertation on "unproductive persistence" found that a third of students who eventually completed assignments experienced extended stuck states. The behaviors that predicted escape from these states were systematic code tracing and organized approaches; unsystematic tinkering kept students trapped.

Feedback design matters considerably. Becker et al.'s comprehensive 2019 ITiCSE Working Group report synthesized 300+ publications on error messages, concluding they remain "frequently inadequate" despite decades of research. Enhanced error messages—with concrete examples, plain language, and actionable suggestions—reduced error frequencies and repeated error metrics in intervention studies, though effects vary by implementation.

## Fixed mindsets and talent beliefs create self-fulfilling prophecies

The belief that programming ability is innate rather than developed through effort consistently predicts poor outcomes. Murphy and Thomas's 2008 foundational paper established that fixed-mindset students exhibit "helpless responses" to substantial challenges—decreased self-esteem, attribution of failure to lack of ability, and reduced effort. Growth-mindset students welcome challenges and maintain self-esteem by attributing setbacks to insufficient effort or strategy.

Scott and Ghinea's 2014 IEEE Transactions on Education study found not only that growth mindset predicted better performance but that **without intervention, student mindsets became more fixed over the duration of programming courses**. This represents a dangerous feedback loop: struggling creates fixed-mindset beliefs, which reduce productive effort, which creates more struggling.

The "geek gene" belief—that some people are simply born to program—persists despite evidence against it. Patitsas, Berlin, Craig, and Easterbrook's 2016 ICER paper analyzed 778 CS grade distributions and found only **5.8% were bimodal**, contrary to widespread instructor belief in a "bimodal split" between those who "get it" and those who don't. Instructors who agreed with "geek gene" statements were more likely to perceive normal distributions as bimodal—confirmation bias masquerading as assessment. Most troubling: Lewis's 2007 survey found **77% of CS faculty strongly disagreed** that "nearly everyone is capable of succeeding in the computer science curriculum if they work at it." Faculty beliefs create self-fulfilling prophecies when they shape course design and support provision.

Help-seeking patterns distinguish successful from struggling learners in complex ways. Research in ACM TOCE (2024) documented that students often avoid seeking help despite available resources—particularly those who don't identify as "CS people" or lack prior experience. These students feel unqualified to approach instructors. Demographic factors interact: Latinx/a/o students and non-CS majors rely more on sources outside the course; first-year students underutilize course-affiliated resources compared to upperclass peers. Unproductive help-seeking—copy-paste without understanding—represents another failure mode documented by Marwan, Dombe, and Price (2020).

Response to errors most clearly separates learning trajectories. Kafai, DeLiema, and colleagues' 2019 SIGCSE paper argued that debugging should be reframed as "productive failure"—an opportunity for learning rather than evidence of inadequacy. Fixed-mindset students see bugs as tests of intelligence; growth-mindset students see them as normal feedback. Kapur's productive failure framework explains why initial struggle often predicts better long-term outcomes: grappling with difficulty builds more robust understanding than smooth success.

Belongingness profoundly affects persistence. Mooney and Becker's 2020 research found CS students have **statistically significantly lower belongingness than other science students**, with intersectional effects—women who also identified as minorities experienced the largest deficits. Cheryan and colleagues documented that stereotypical cues in CS environments (hacker culture, masculine décor) affect gender participation through "ambient belonging" signals. The culture of computing, as Margolis and Fisher (2002) argued, "contributes to boys' sense of belonging and girls' sense of 'outsidership.'" Good, Rattan, and Dweck (2012) demonstrated that growth mindset messaging can protect women from stereotype threat effects, maintaining belongingness and intention to continue.

## Practical implications for curriculum design and classroom intervention

The research synthesis points toward specific, actionable recommendations for supporting struggling learners in introductory through intermediate programming.

**Sequence instruction from reading to writing.** Design courses that begin topics with code prediction and tracing before requiring generation. Implement the PRIMM framework systematically: predict output, run to verify, investigate through annotation, modify existing code, then create new programs. Budget 20-30 minutes of initial tracing activities—this directly reduces dropout and improves achievement.

**Scaffold the generation gap.** Use Parsons problems as an intermediate step between reading and writing code. Their equal effectiveness with 30-50% faster completion makes them efficient learning activities. Consider adaptive versions that adjust difficulty based on student responses. Combine with subgoal labels in worked examples, particularly for complex topics like object-oriented design—this disproportionately helps at-risk students.

**Restructure social learning.** Implement peer instruction with conceptual multiple-choice questions that reveal misconceptions. The individual-vote-discuss-revote-explain sequence activates peer learning mechanisms that correlate with halved failure rates. Use pair programming selectively for complex, unfamiliar tasks where the quality benefit outweighs effort costs; it particularly helps novice-novice pairs maintain confidence.

**Teach debugging explicitly.** Provide context-specific debugging instruction with concrete examples rather than abstract guidelines alone. Students need to see worked debugging examples, not just general strategies. Identify unproductive persistence early—students making trivial, unsystematic edits need intervention toward organized tracing approaches.

**Address mindsets directly.** Explicitly counter the geek gene myth with evidence about the role of practice and prior experience (not innate talent) in programming success. Normalize struggle as part of learning—the research on productive confusion suggests brief struggle is beneficial. Design mastery experiences that build self-efficacy; even brief hands-on successes can close demographic gaps in confidence.

**Cultivate belonging.** Examine classroom environments for signals that might exclude students who don't identify with hacker stereotypes. Provide diverse role models and acknowledge varied paths into computing. Address imposter syndrome explicitly given its 57% prevalence. Support help-seeking through multiple channels—students from different backgrounds prefer different resources.

**Improve feedback systems.** Enhance error messages with plain language explanations, concrete examples, and actionable suggestions. Consider that repeated encounters with the same error indicate conceptual struggles requiring intervention, not just more practice. Design feedback to be specific and adaptive to individual learner states.

The overarching finding across this literature is that programming difficulty is not fixed—it reflects instructional design, belief systems, and support structures that can all be modified. The approximately 50% failure rate in introductory programming is not inevitable; institutions implementing evidence-based interventions have achieved substantial reductions. The path forward requires treating programming education as a design problem amenable to systematic improvement rather than a filter that naturally selects for talent.
